\begin{abstract}
Ge'ez-script languages are morphologically rich, and standard subword
tokenizers routinely place boundaries inside morphemes. We present MoVoC, a
morphology-aware vocabulary construction method that reserves half of its
budget for morpheme types recovered by supervised morphological analysis and
half for BPE merges, together with MoVoC-Tok, a constrained-merge tokenizer in
which no merge may cross a morpheme boundary. We release morpheme annotations
for Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre and Ge'ez comprising 169,806 records, of which
129,261 carry at least one morpheme boundary; three of these four languages had
no prior public morpheme-annotated resource. We evaluate intrinsically with
morpheme boundary precision, MorphScore and R\'enyi entropy, scoring every
in-language comparison on words held out from the morpheme resource that
constrains MoVoC-Tok's own training. On held-out words MoVoC-Tok attains the
highest MorphScore in three of four languages and the highest morpheme boundary
precision in two of four, with its largest and most clearly resolved gains on
Tigre, a language for which it has no training data and to which it is applied
cross-lingually. We release the annotations, vocabularies and evaluation code
as a reference implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ge'ez-script languages---Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre and Ge'ez---are
morphologically rich. A single orthographic word may carry prefixes, a root,
optional infixes, suffixes and clitics. Standard subword tokenizers are trained
on frequency statistics alone and place boundaries wherever the corpus
statistics suggest, so a token can straddle a prefix--root or root--suffix
join. The resulting vocabulary encodes fragments that do not correspond to
linguistic units.

The writing system compounds the difficulty. Ge'ez script is an abugida: one
character encodes a consonant--vowel pair, so morphological structure and
orthographic units do not align one-to-one. A morpheme boundary can fall inside
a single character.

These languages are also low-resource, with limited morphological annotation
available for supervised segmentation. This combination---rich morphology,
an abugida orthography, and scarce annotation---motivates vocabulary
construction that uses morphological information directly rather than inferring
it from frequency.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{MoVoC}, a hybrid vocabulary construction method that allocates
        half the vocabulary budget to morpheme types and half to BPE merges
        (Section~\ref{sec:movoc}).
  \item \textbf{MoVoC-Tok}, a constrained-merge tokenizer in which no merge
        crosses a morpheme boundary (Section~\ref{sec:movoctok}).
  \item \textbf{Morpheme-annotated datasets} for four Ge'ez-script languages,
        169,806 records in total (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}).
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic evaluation} using morpheme boundary precision,
        MorphScore and R\'enyi entropy, scored on words held out from the
        morpheme resource that constrains MoVoC-Tok's training
        (Section~\ref{sec:intrinsic}). The downstream translation evaluation
        reported in the published version is withdrawn
        (Section~\ref{sec:extrinsic}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Subword tokenization.}
Byte-Pair Encoding \citep{sennrich2016bpe} merges frequent character pairs and
is the baseline throughout this work. WordPiece \citep{schuster2012wordpiece}
selects merges by likelihood rather than raw frequency. SentencePiece and its
Unigram model \citep{kudo2018sentencepiece} remove the pre-tokenization step
entirely. These methods share a property: boundaries are induced from corpus
statistics with no morphological constraint, which is precisely what leads them
to split inside morphemes in morphologically rich languages.

\paragraph{Morphology-aware segmentation.}
Morfessor \citep{creutz2007morfessor} induces morphological segmentation
without supervision. HornMorpho \citep{gasser2011hornmorpho} provides supervised
morphological analysis for Ethiopian Semitic languages and is the analyzer
underlying our annotation pipeline.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Morpheme boundary precision \citep{nouri2016precision} compares predicted
boundaries against gold morpheme boundaries. MorphScore
\citep{arnett2025morphscore} is recall-oriented, counting gold boundaries the
tokenizer recovers and excluding words left unsegmented. R\'enyi entropy
\citep{renyi1961entropy} characterises the diversity of the resulting token
distribution, with lower values indicating sharper, more consistent
segmentation. We report all three, since they capture complementary failure
modes.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Pre-tokenization and morphological analysis}

Words are analysed into \texttt{(prefix, root, infix, suffix, clitic)} slots by
supervised morphological analysis, with human post-editing for Amharic and
Tigrinya and manual annotation for Tigre and Ge'ez.

\subsection{MoVoC vocabulary construction}
\label{sec:movoc}

The vocabulary budget is split evenly between morpheme types and BPE merges.
For a total size $s$ and morpheme ratio $r$:
%
\begin{align}
  s_{\text{lang}}     &= s / 2 \\
  s_{\text{morpheme}} &= s_{\text{lang}} \times r \\
  s_{\text{BPE}}      &= s_{\text{lang}} \times (1 - r)
\end{align}
%
Morphemes enter the vocabulary as first-class units rather than being
discovered statistically, so frequent affixes and roots survive as whole
tokens. The remaining budget is filled by ordinary BPE merges, which retain
coverage for material the morphological analyser does not segment.

\subsection{MoVoC-Tok}
\label{sec:movoctok}

MoVoC-Tok is a constrained-merge BPE: \emph{a merge may never cross a morpheme
boundary.} Segmentation therefore stays inside morphemes, and a token cannot
straddle a prefix--root or root--suffix join. Within each morpheme, merges
proceed by the usual BPE ranking, so the tokenizer remains a deterministic,
greedy, linear-time segmenter.

evaluation, as no separate training morpheme data was obtained for them.

Parallel evaluation data comprises per-language test sets together with
FLORES-200.

\section{Intrinsic Evaluation}
\label{sec:intrinsic}

\subsection{Metrics}

Boundary positions are derived as cumulative morpheme lengths. We report
morpheme boundary precision (exact character-offset match of predicted against
gold boundaries, micro-averaged), MorphScore (recall of gold boundaries, with
unsegmented words excluded rather than scored zero), and R\'enyi entropy at
$\alpha = 2$, normalized as $H_\alpha / \log(\text{support})$ so that values
fall in $[0, 1]$.

\subsection{Held-out evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:heldout}

MoVoC-Tok is a supervised tokenizer: it consumes morpheme annotations that BPE
and WordPiece do not receive. Using that supervision is the method, and a gain
obtained from it is a result. It is a result, however, only on words the
supervision was not given for. Part of each in-language evaluation set is drawn
from the same morpheme resource that constrained MoVoC-Tok's training, and on
those words the tokenizer reproduces a constraint it was handed rather than
generalizing from it, while the baselines meet the words for the first time.

We therefore exclude, per language, every evaluation word occurring in the
morpheme resource named as \texttt{morpheme\_source} in that tokenizer's
training configuration. This removes 4,736 of 81,224 Amharic words (5.8\%) and
2,786 of 5,224 Tigrinya words (53.3\%), leaving 76,488 and 2,438. Exclusion is
by set membership against the resource itself rather than by the evaluation
set's provenance labels, which record only the first source each word was drawn
from and so undercount the overlap. Tigre and Ge'ez have no training morpheme
resource of their own, but the tokenizer that scores them does: they are
evaluated cross-lingually with the Tigrinya \movoctok{}, so the same exclusion
applies against the Tigrinya resource, removing 187 of 1,974 Tigre words
(9.5\%) and 2 of 172 Ge'ez words and leaving 1,787 and 170.

All intrinsic results below are reported on held-out words. Differences are
assessed with a paired bootstrap over words (10,000 resamples, resampling index
shared across tokenizers), reported in Table~\ref{tab:significance}.

\paragraph{Precision.}
On held-out words MoVoC-Tok attains the highest boundary precision against BPE
in two of four languages: Amharic ($+0.0018$, $p = 0.012$) and Tigre
($+0.0250$, $p = 0.009$). Against WordPiece the Amharic margin is an order of
magnitude larger ($+0.0176$, $p < 0.001$). On Tigrinya the difference against
BPE is $-0.0016$ with a 95\% interval of $[-0.0106, +0.0075]$: the held-out
data do not separate the two tokenizers on this metric, and we claim no
precision gain for Tigrinya. On Ge'ez the two are close to tied ($-0.0025$,
$p = 0.839$) on 172 words, which likewise resolves nothing in either
direction.

The Tigrinya precision result is the one place where a conclusion changes under
the held-out protocol. Over the full evaluation set, including the words
MoVoC-Tok was trained on, it leads (0.3242 against 0.3142); over held-out words
alone it does not. We read this as a statement about annotation coverage rather
than about the method, and return to it in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Precision is markedly higher for Tigre than for the more fusional languages.
Tigre's gold annotations are entirely surface-concatenative, so morpheme
boundaries coincide exactly with character offsets and the boundary
projection introduces no approximation; this reflects how morphological
structure maps onto the abugida orthography and affects all three
tokenizers similarly.

Both Table~\ref{tab:datasets} and Table~\ref{tab:intrinsic} report values well
below the originally published record's exact figures (precision 73.9--88.3
there against 0.2385--0.5629 here, over a different evaluation set). Neither
table reproduces those numbers, nor is intended to.

\section{Extrinsic Evaluation Withdrawn}
\label{sec:extrinsic}

The published version reported BLEU and chrF++ for English$\rightarrow$X
translation with each tokenizer, from multilingual MarianMT models trained one
per tokenizer over three seeds. Those results are withdrawn, for three
independent reasons.

\paragraph{The outputs are unavailable.} Every one of the 54 decode passes
recorded for those runs references a hypothesis and reference file that is not
present in the released repository or its history. The reported scores cannot
be checked against the translations they summarise.

\paragraph{Generation did not terminate correctly.} The recorded
hypothesis-to-reference length ratios run 2.06--4.28 on FLORES-200 and as high
as 15.10 on the smaller OPUS sets. Decoding is reaching the 128-token cap
instead of emitting end-of-sequence. This is not a training-budget effect:
final training loss across the runs is 2.9967--3.5854, the same range as a
comparable from-scratch MarianMT that reached 24.7 BLEU
English$\rightarrow$Amharic at loss 3.13. A comparable loss with BLEU below
1.56 in every cell indicates a fault in generation or scoring rather than
insufficient training.

\paragraph{The configuration cannot be established.} The surviving records
disagree on decoding: one gives five-beam search, another greedy decoding with
a single beam. Whether the models were initialised from a pretrained MarianMT
checkpoint or trained from scratch is likewise unresolved. A corrected paper
cannot publish a results table whose experimental setup it is unable to
describe.

We therefore make no extrinsic claim. MoVoC's contribution is a tokenization
method and an annotated dataset, and we evaluate it intrinsically. Whether
morphology-aware tokenization improves downstream translation for these
languages remains an open question, and answering it requires a corrected
generation pipeline rather than a re-scoring of these runs.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Cross-lingual transfer is the clearest intrinsic result.}
The largest and most clearly resolved gains in Table~\ref{tab:intrinsic} are on
Tigre ($+0.0250$ precision, $p = 0.009$; $+0.0274$ MorphScore, $p = 0.003$,
both against BPE), a language with no training morpheme resource of its own, to
which the Tigrinya MoVoC-Tok is applied cross-lingually. This is the cleanest
evidence available here that the method works as intended: the constraint that
merges respect morpheme boundaries yields a vocabulary whose units align with
morpheme structure in a language the supervision never covered, which is what a
morphology-aware tokenizer is supposed to do and what no amount of in-language
overlap could explain.

\paragraph{Supervision helps where annotation coverage is sufficient.}
The two in-language results differ, and the difference tracks annotation
coverage rather than anything about the languages' morphology. The Amharic
morpheme vocabulary realises 50,978 types from 10,293 training records; the
Tigrinya vocabulary realises 7,125 from 6,692. On Amharic, MoVoC-Tok leads on
both metrics over 76,488 held-out words. On Tigrinya its MorphScore advantage
survives the held-out protocol ($+0.0185$, $p = 0.017$) while its precision
advantage does not ($-0.0016$, $[-0.0106, +0.0075]$). That the recall-oriented
metric improves while the precision metric does not is consistent with a
constraint that reaches too few word types: the learned merges still recover
gold boundaries where they fire, but do not fire on enough unseen forms to
raise precision overall. Morpheme supervision helps where annotation coverage
is sufficient; our Amharic and Tigre results indicate what sufficient looks
like, and our Tigrinya result marks where coverage falls short.

\paragraph{Boundary precision tracks morphological regularity.}
MoVoC-Tok attains its highest boundary precision in Tigre, the language whose
annotations are fully surface-concatenative. That the clearest precision
advantage appears where the orthography aligns most directly with morpheme
structure is consistent with the method operating as intended, though it also
means the Tigre result benefits from an annotation style that introduces no
boundary-projection approximation, and is to that extent a favourable case. The
generally
higher precision for Tigre across all three tokenizers indicates that a
substantial part of the difficulty in the other languages arises from the
mapping between morphology and abugida orthography rather than from any one
tokenizer.

\paragraph{We make no claim about downstream behaviour.}
The extrinsic evaluation is withdrawn (Section~\ref{sec:extrinsic}), so this
paper offers no evidence on whether morphology-aware tokenization helps or
hurts translation for these languages. We note only that intrinsic segmentation
quality and downstream translation quality are distinct measurements and that
neither should be treated as a proxy for the other; establishing the
relationship between them here would require a working generation pipeline,
which we do not have.

\paragraph{The metrics are complementary.}
Boundary precision penalises spurious boundaries; MorphScore is recall-oriented
and does not penalise false positives. A tokenizer that under-segments scores
well on precision, and one that over-segments scores well on recall. Reporting
them together characterises segmentation behaviour more completely than either
alone.

\paragraph{The annotations as a contribution.}
The released annotations support both the vocabulary construction method and
the intrinsic evaluation, in a single documented schema across four languages,
three of which had no prior public morpheme-annotated resource.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Held-out evaluation and its cost.}
All in-language intrinsic results are reported on words held out from the
morpheme resource that constrained MoVoC-Tok's training
(Section~\ref{sec:heldout}). We did not re-derive those resources to enlarge
the held-out sets, so excluding the overlap leaves Tigrinya with 2,438
evaluation words, and its confidence intervals are correspondingly wide. On
Tigrinya the boundary precision advantage does not survive the exclusion, and
we claim no precision gain there.

\paragraph{The in-language margins over BPE are small.}
On Amharic, the largest held-out evaluation set at 76,488 words, MoVoC-Tok's
advantage over BPE is statistically resolved but small in absolute terms
($+0.0018$ precision, $+0.0021$ MorphScore). We do not claim these margins are
of practical consequence on their own. The margins over WordPiece are
substantially larger ($+0.0176$ and $+0.0276$), as is the cross-lingual Tigre
result, and those carry correspondingly more weight.

\paragraph{Annotation coverage varies by two orders of magnitude.}
Amharic contributes 153,759 records while Ge'ez contributes 193. Intrinsic
results for Ge'ez rest on 172 evaluation words and do not resolve differences
between tokenizers in either direction (Table~\ref{tab:significance}); they
should not be read as evidence for or against any of them.

\paragraph{Cross-lingual application for Tigre and Ge'ez.}
No in-language MoVoC-Tok exists for Tigre or Ge'ez, as no separate training
morpheme data was obtained for them and their annotations are reserved for
evaluation. Their rows in Table~\ref{tab:intrinsic} apply the Tigrinya
MoVoC-Tok cross-lingually.

\paragraph{No downstream evaluation.}
The extrinsic evaluation is withdrawn (Section~\ref{sec:extrinsic}), so the
method is assessed only by how well its segmentations align with gold morpheme
boundaries. Boundary precision and MorphScore measure that alignment directly,
but they do not establish that better-aligned segmentation improves any
downstream task, and we do not claim it does. The practical value of
morphology-aware tokenization for these languages is untested here.

\paragraph{Boundary metrics require surface-aligned annotations.}
Boundary precision and MorphScore locate gold boundaries by character offset,
so they apply to annotations whose morphemes concatenate to the surface form.
This constrains the evaluable subset in the more fusional languages.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented MoVoC, a morphology-aware vocabulary construction method for
Ge'ez-script languages, and MoVoC-Tok, its constrained-merge tokenizer, along
with morpheme annotations for Amharic, Tigrinya, Tigre and Ge'ez. Scoring every
in-language comparison on words held out from the morpheme resource that
constrains its own training, MoVoC-Tok attains the highest MorphScore in three
of four languages and the highest boundary precision in two of four. Its
largest gains are on Tigre, which it was not trained on and to which it is
applied cross-lingually, indicating that constraining subword merges to
morpheme boundaries produces segmentation that aligns with morpheme structure
beyond the words the supervision covered. Where coverage is thinner, the effect
is weaker: on Tigrinya, whose morpheme vocabulary realises 7,125 types against
50,978 for Amharic, the MorphScore advantage persists on held-out words while
the precision advantage does not. The downstream translation evaluation is
withdrawn, so we make no claim about the method's effect on translation
quality.

Future work includes training in-language MoVoC-Tok models for Tigre and Ge'ez
given sufficient annotation, extending supervised translation coverage beyond
Amharic and Tigrinya, and testing at larger translation training scales whether
the intrinsic advantage transfers downstream.

We release the annotations, vocabularies and evaluation code as a reference
implementation.
